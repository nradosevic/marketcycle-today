Why Inflation Expectations Matter More Than Inflation Itself

By Dr. Christina Park • March 12, 2026

Central bankers speak obsessively about "anchored inflation expectations," and for good reason: economic research consistently demonstrates that what people expect to happen with prices often matters more for economic outcomes than what's actually happening with prices. The distinction may seem academic, but for investors navigating an era of elevated and volatile inflation, understanding expectations dynamics provides critical insight into both central bank behavior and asset price implications.

The mechanism works through self-fulfilling prophecy. When workers expect prices to rise, they demand higher wages. When businesses expect input costs to increase, they raise prices preemptively. When investors expect purchasing power to erode, they demand higher nominal returns. These behavioral responses can transform expected inflation into actual inflation, creating feedback loops that prove difficult to break once established. The inflationary spirals of the 1970s demonstrated how unanchored expectations could become entrenched, requiring painful economic adjustment to reset.

Measuring inflation expectations requires examining multiple indicators, each with strengths and limitations. Survey-based measures ask consumers and professionals directly about their expectations; the University of Michigan Consumer Survey and the Survey of Professional Forecasters are widely followed examples. Market-based measures derive expectations from the pricing of inflation-protected securities relative to nominal bonds. The "breakeven" inflation rate—the difference between nominal Treasury yields and TIPS yields—provides real-time market signals, though it also incorporates liquidity and risk premiums that can distort the expectations signal.

The Federal Reserve monitors expectations with particular intensity because its credibility depends on keeping them stable. If market participants and consumers believe the Fed will achieve its 2% inflation target over time, temporary inflation overshoots cause less economic disruption. Wages and prices adjust more smoothly, and long-term investment decisions don't get distorted by anticipated purchasing power erosion. Conversely, if expectations become de-anchored—if people start believing higher inflation is permanent—the Fed may need to induce significant economic pain to restore credibility, as Paul Volcker demonstrated in the early 1980s.

The post-pandemic inflation episode provided a real-world test of expectations anchoring. Despite inflation reaching 40-year highs in 2022, longer-term expectations remained relatively stable. Five-year, five-year forward inflation expectations—a measure of what markets expect inflation to average between five and ten years in the future—never exceeded 2.5%, suggesting confidence that elevated inflation would prove transitory even as current readings surged. This anchoring likely contributed to the relatively smooth disinflation that followed, validating the Fed's initial patience.

For investors, expectations provide signals that complement traditional inflation metrics. When breakeven inflation rates rise sharply, inflation-protected securities may warrant increased allocation. When consumer expectations diverge from professional forecasts, it often signals near-term inflation surprises that markets haven't fully priced. And when longer-term expectations shift meaningfully, it typically precedes significant changes in Fed policy and thus affects asset prices across markets. The expectations component of inflation analysis is as important as the inflation data itself.

Looking at 2026, inflation expectations have moderated to levels consistent with the Fed's framework, providing policy flexibility that wasn't available when expectations showed signs of de-anchoring. However, structural factors—from supply chain reconfiguration to energy transition to demographic shifts—create ongoing uncertainty about the inflation regime. Investors should monitor expectations measures alongside actual inflation, recognizing that the interplay between the two will continue to drive both central bank decisions and market outcomes in the years ahead.
